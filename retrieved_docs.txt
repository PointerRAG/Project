Diabetes Mellitus - Introduction and Epidemiology Diabetes mellitus is a chronic, non-communicable metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia. It arises due to defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. According to the World Health Organization, diabetes is one of the fastest growing global health emergencies of the 21st century. The global prevalence of diabetes has risen dramatically over the past decades due to urbanization, sedentary lifestyles, dietary changes, and increasing obesity rates. Both developed and developing nations are affected, with a disproportionate burden observed in low- and middle-income countries. Diabetes significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, blindness, and lower-limb amputations

Types of Diabetes Mellitus Type 1 diabetes mellitus is an autoimmune condition resulting in the destruction of insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas. It typically presents in childhood or adolescence, although adult-onset cases are increasingly recognized. Patients require lifelong insulin therapy for survival. Type 2 diabetes mellitus is the most common form and is characterized by insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency. Genetic predisposition combined with lifestyle factors such as obesity and physical inactivity play a major role. Gestational diabetes occurs during pregnancy and increases the risk of future type 2 diabetes for both mother and child. Other less common forms include monogenic diabetes, pancreatic diabetes, and drug-induced diabetes.

Pathophysiology of Diabetes Mellitus In type 1 diabetes, autoimmune destruction of beta cells leads to absolute insulin deficiency. The absence of insulin prevents glucose uptake by peripheral tissues, resulting in hyperglycemia and ketosis. In type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance in muscle, liver, and adipose tissue is the primary defect. The pancreas initially compensates by increasing insulin secretion; however, beta-cell dysfunction eventually develops, leading to sustained hyperglycemia. Chronic hyperglycemia causes non-enzymatic glycation of proteins, oxidative stress, and inflammation, which contribute to long-term vascular complications.
